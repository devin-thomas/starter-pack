# Build Something Meaningful

Build a small project you would actually use: a personal tracker, a searchable collection, a generator, or a prototype of a larger idea. Choose something you can finish. Save your most ambitious long-term project for the next phase.

## Bring your progress with you

Open your harness on a computer, or use an already reliable remote connection. Copy the Phase 2 handoff below into your agent, then attach your Phase 1 progress file if you have one. If you saved your progress as text, paste that too. If you have neither, tell the agent what you completed so it can rebuild the record with you.

Before starting computer setup, have the agent create or confirm a **private GitHub progress repository** for your progress, computer setup status, and project notes. The handoff points it to the template it needs. This website does not store your completion state; your agent keeps that record in files you own.

**Google users:** bring this handoff and your saved Gemini progress into Antigravity on your computer, or into an existing Antigravity session through its browser connection. Gemini and Antigravity are separate; carry your progress over rather than assuming the conversation has transferred.

## Complete Computer Setup

Tell your agent: **Run Starter Pack Computer Setup.** It reads the Computer Setup instructions from the handoff and inspects your operating system and installed tools before proposing changes. Windows 11 and current macOS are first-class paths; Linux support is best effort.

Your agent presents one plain-English plan for the required tools and asks for batch approval. Existing working tools count. It pauses when you need to sign in, approve administrator access, or restart, and saves enough nonsecret state to resume. Optional tools are separate choices. Docker Desktop is only for projects that need containers and is installed last.

The baseline includes a harness, Git and GitHub CLI, Node LTS, Python through uv, search and JSON utilities, and Tailscale. You do not need to memorize these names or install every framework globally. The agent verifies that the tools work and records the results.

## Connect from your phone once

Install and sign in to Tailscale on your computer and phone. It provides a private network and device names. You also need a way to control the harness: a browser connection, a harness-native remote client, a suitable remote desktop, or SSH.

**Antigravity users:** follow the [Remote Control setup](https://antigravity.google/docs/remote-control/), then open [the dashboard](https://antigravity.google.com/) in your phone's browser. Use the same Google account as the computer instance, select that computer, and send a message in its agent session. The computer must be awake and connected. Your agent checks Tailscale separately; Antigravity's dashboard handles its own connection.

For a connection over Tailscale, use the computer's Tailscale/MagicDNS name. In either case, complete one simple interaction with the actual harness and tell your agent what worked. Installing Tailscale or chatting in Gemini alone does not complete this step. If connection fails, keep the successful setup results and record the exact resume point. The computer setup milestone is complete after the required capabilities and this phone connection work, and the state is saved to your private progress repo.

<h3 id="phase-2-start-with-an-interest">An interest is enough of a starting point</h3>

I wanted a useful reason to revisit fighting games. The idea behind Daily Combo Trials was simple: pick a game, pick a character, and give me a combo-trial challenge. I already cared about the collection. The useful thing to build was a way to make one decision from it.

Steam API data was part of how I started. An API is a way for software to request information or actions from another service. An exported library and a curated set of games became a catalog that I could keep with the project. The app did not need to sign everyone into Steam or continually synchronize their accounts to make that catalog useful. I could review the starting material, choose what belonged, and build around it.

</section>

<section class="field-lesson" aria-labelledby="phase-2-find-your-question">

<p class="eyebrow">Try it in your project</p>

<h3 id="phase-2-find-your-question">Find one question in your collection</h3>

For your project, look at information you already keep: a reading list, practice notes, a collection, or equipment you own. Pick one question you repeatedly ask of it. "What should I practice today?" is a much clearer starting point than "Build me a platform for my hobby."

Before connecting a service, ask whether a permitted export or a small, reviewed sample would be enough. A snapshot fits a collection that changes occasionally. Current availability or each person's live account data would be a different promise and would need a different plan.

> Tell your agent: "Here is a collection I care about. Help me choose one useful question it could answer. Identify the few fields we need and whether a reviewed snapshot is enough for the first version."

</section>

## Have a short design conversation

Tell your agent: **Run Quick Build for my idea.** It will ask one compact round about what you want to make, who it is for, the main actions, your existing content, what to leave out, how it should feel, and what would count as done. Answer conversationally; you can leave routine technical choices to the agent.

Your agent reads the Quick Build instructions from the handoff and turns that conversation into a plan, a specification, decisions, and ordered build tasks. It creates and maintains those files. Ask to see them whenever useful; your next action is to review the agent's summary of the intended result, then say **go** or request a revision.

<section class="field-lesson" aria-labelledby="phase-2-say-the-rule">

<p class="eyebrow">From my projects · Daily Combo Trials</p>

<h3 id="phase-2-say-the-rule">Explain what a good result means</h3>

"Pick a character" leaves a surprising amount undecided. In Daily Combo Trials, the rule is to choose a game first, then a character from that game. Choosing from one giant list of characters would give games with larger rosters more chances to appear. Both approaches can produce a random character; they express different ideas about the daily choice.

I also kept the shared daily challenge separate from a visitor's reroll. Someone can ask for another suggestion without changing what everyone else sees for the day. Those are small sentences, but they determine what the buttons should do and what the agent should verify.

My original project used three discovery rounds. Quick Build gives you one compact round for this phase; the example shows the kinds of decisions to make, without asking you to repeat my entire process.

</section>

<section class="field-lesson" aria-labelledby="phase-2-describe-the-results">

<p class="eyebrow">Try it in your project</p>

<h3 id="phase-2-describe-the-results">Describe the results you expect</h3>

Try writing two or three examples before approving your build. Describe an ordinary result, something that should stay unchanged, and an outcome that would feel wrong. You do not need to name an algorithm. Your job is to explain the behavior well enough that you can recognize it when you try the app.

> Ask your agent: "Write the main rule in plain language. Show me one normal example and one surprising edge case. Turn the rule into checks I can understand."

</section>

## Build and put it online

Once you approve, the agent implements the smallest complete version in a durable project repository. Cloudflare is the default deployment platform. Vercel is an optional alternative when suitable. Neon comes into the build only if the project needs persistent relational data. Accounts, payments, and integrations are added only when they serve the product you approved.

Review the main behavior yourself and tell the agent what needs changing. Verification should fit the project and your testing preferences. Then deploy the project and open its live URL. A successful local build alone does not show that a deployed app works: try the main action at that live URL.

<section class="field-lesson" aria-labelledby="phase-2-save-what-matters">

<p class="eyebrow">From my projects · Daily Combo Trials</p>

<h3 id="phase-2-save-what-matters">Decide what has to survive</h3>

I used different storage for different promises. The catalog lives with the project. Daily assignments are stored so that an old recommendation stays the same even after the catalog changes. A visitor's reroll is temporary browser state. Accounts and completion tracking were outside the scope of this build.

</section>

<section class="field-lesson" aria-labelledby="phase-2-check-what-survives">

<p class="eyebrow">Try it in your project</p>

<h3 id="phase-2-check-what-survives">Check what your app will remember</h3>

That distinction gives you a useful review exercise. List the information in your app, then ask who needs to see it and how long it must last. A temporary choice, shared history, and a person's saved progress are different requirements. Tell your agent which one you mean before it chooses storage.

My project used Python and deployed on Vercel, with Supabase storing production history. Your build can use Starter Pack's defaults and still apply these decisions. The question to carry forward is what your product must remember. Once it is online, check that the live version keeps that promise.

> Ask your agent: "What will survive a refresh, another device, or a new deployment? Show me how we will verify the parts my app promises to remember at its live URL."

</section>

## Finish with something you can return to

Your finish line is a meaningful working project, source saved in a durable repository, a live deployment with its primary flow verified, and an updated private progress record. Keep the repository and live links with the result. If deployment or the live action still fails, record the remaining work and resume there rather than marking the phase complete.

When that is done, look ahead to [Build Something Serious](/phases/3).
